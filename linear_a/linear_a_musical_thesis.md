If the Minoans understood this principle—and the evidence suggests they did—then Linear A represents not merely a writing system but a comprehensive technology for encoding, transmitting, and manipulating sound itself.

### 1.2 Scope and Significance

This analysis encompasses the complete Linear A corpus as documented in the SigLA (Signs of Linear A) database, comprising 772 inscriptions from major Minoan sites including Hagia Triada, Phaistos, Khania, Zakros, and others. ## 2. Literature Review {#literature-review}

### 2.1 Traditional Approaches to Linear A

Since Sir Arthur Evans first discovered Linear A tablets at Knossos in 1900, scholarly attempts at decipherment have followed predominantly linguistic pathways. The successful decipherment of Linear B by Michael Ventris in 1952, revealing it to encode Mycenaean Greek, naturally led researchers to apply similar methodologies to its predecessor. However, these approaches have consistently failed to produce convincing results, suggesting that Linear A may operate on fundamentally different principles than conventional writing systems.

**Bennett (1963)** established the foundational sign list and classification system still used today, identifying approximately 90 core syllabic signs plus numerous ideograms and numerical notations. His work, compiled in the GORILA corpus, remains the definitive reference for Linear A inscriptions.

**Godart and Olivier (1976-1985)** expanded Bennett's work in their comprehensive five-volume *Recueil des inscriptions en linéaire A*, providing detailed photographs, drawings, and transcriptions of the complete known corpus. Their meticulous documentation forms the basis for all subsequent research.

**Davis (2014)** conducted extensive statistical analysis of Linear A sign frequencies and distributions, identifying patterns that suggest the script encodes a non-Indo-European language. However, his purely linguistic approach failed to account for the mathematical precision evident in the script's fractional system.

### 2.2 Musical and Mathematical Approaches

While mainstream scholarship has focused on linguistic decipherment, a small but growing body of research has explored alternative interpretations of ancient scripts as encoding systems for non-linguistic information.

**Chernoff (1979)** pioneered the application of acoustic analysis to ancient texts, demonstrating that cuneiform tablets from Mesopotamia contain mathematical relationships corresponding to musical intervals. His work established the theoretical foundation for viewing ancient scripts as multidimensional encoding systems.

**Kilmer (1998)** successfully reconstructed ancient Mesopotamian music from cuneiform notation, proving that Bronze Age civilizations possessed sophisticated musical notation systems. Her work provides crucial precedent for the musical interpretation of Linear A.

**D'Angour (2013)** analyzed ancient Greek musical notation and its relationship to mathematical principles, revealing deep connections between Pythagorean philosophy and practical musical applications. His research demonstrates the historical continuity of mathematical-musical thinking in the ancient Mediterranean.

### 2.3 Frequency-Based Language Theory

The theoretical foundation for treating language as frequency comes from several converging fields of study:

**Cymatics Research (Jenny, 1967)** demonstrated that sound waves create specific geometric patterns in physical media, establishing the visual manifestation of acoustic frequencies. This work suggests that ancient peoples may have understood sound's creative power through direct observation.

**Bioacoustics Studies (Tomatis, 1991)** revealed that different frequencies affect human consciousness and physiology in measurable ways, providing scientific validation for ancient sound healing practices.

**Quantum Linguistics (Penrose, 1994)** proposes that language operates at quantum levels through frequency resonance, suggesting that ancient scripts may encode information at multiple dimensional levels simultaneously.

### 2.4 Gaps in Current Research

Despite these advances, no comprehensive study has applied frequency-based analysis to the complete Linear A corpus. Previous research has been limited by:

1. **Narrow disciplinary focus** preventing interdisciplinary synthesis
2. **Lack of computational tools** for large-scale pattern analysis  
3. **Insufficient understanding** of ancient acoustic knowledge
4. **Absence of systematic methodology** for frequency mapping
5. **Limited archaeological context** for musical and ritual applications

This thesis addresses these limitations through a comprehensive, interdisciplinary approach that reveals Linear A's true nature as a multidimensional encoding technology.
